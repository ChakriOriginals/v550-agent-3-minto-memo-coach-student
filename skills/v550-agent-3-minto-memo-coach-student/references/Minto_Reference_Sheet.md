# Minto Pyramid Principle — V550 reference sheet

Lead with the answer, support it with reasons, and back the reasons with evidence.

## Three levels

| Level | V550 term | Reading synonym | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Top | Main Idea | The answer | One sentence that directly answers the reader's question or states the recommendation. |
| Middle | Key Arguments | Supporting arguments | Reasons the Main Idea holds; each answers “why?” or “how?” |
| Bottom | Evidence | Supporting data or facts | Specific facts, numbers, dates, and examples supporting each Key Argument. |

Each Key Argument can itself sit above several pieces of Evidence.

## Four logic principles

- **Answer First:** Put the Main Idea near the start rather than building toward it.
- **Vertical Logic:** A point raises a “why?” or “how?” question that the points beneath it answer.
- **Horizontal Logic:** Points at the same level are the same kind of thing and follow a clear order, such as time, parts of a whole, or importance.
- **MECE:** Mutually Exclusive means no overlap; Collectively Exhaustive means no meaningful gap. Apply this to Key Arguments with judgment, not as a rigid count.

## Think bottom up, communicate top down

To discover the answer, work from Evidence through reasons to the Main Idea. To explain it, give the Main Idea first, then Key Arguments, then Evidence. The reader needs the conclusion before the story of how the work was done.

## SCQA for an opening

- **Situation:** Facts the reader already knows and accepts.
- **Complication:** What changed, or why action is needed.
- **Question:** The decision or problem the Complication creates.
- **Answer:** The Main Idea.

The Question should follow from the Complication. The Answer should resolve the Question. Coach each element separately.

## Inductive and deductive reasoning

- **Inductive:** Several Key Arguments together support the Main Idea. This is usually the clearest starting point; a challenge to one reason need not undo the others.
- **Deductive:** A connected sequence of premise, second premise, and conclusion. It can help bring a skeptical reader along step by step. Let the student choose whether it fits.

For a short client memo, ask whether the opening gives the reader the answer, whether each paragraph supports it, whether claims have specific Evidence, and whether any process detail can be removed.
